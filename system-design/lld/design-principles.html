Design Principles: DRY, KISS, YAGNI and More

  • Every principle answers one question: when a requirement changes, how many classes do I have to edit?
  • DRY: one place per piece of knowledge. Law of Demeter: talk to your friends, not their friends. Program to an interface: depend on what, not who.
  • Composition over inheritance: combine small parts instead of multiplying subclasses.
  • High cohesion, low coupling are the measures; the other principles are ways to improve them.
  • KISS and YAGNI are the brakes: don't add abstractions or features until they earn their place.

3Revise in two minutes

Cheat-sheet

  • The same business rule appears in several places→DRY: give it one home (a class, a constant, a method)
  • Two pieces of code look alike but change for different reasons→leave them apart: that's accidental duplication, not DRY
  • Chains like a.B.C.D.Value→Law of Demeter: ask the first object to do it ("tell, don't ask")
  • new ConcreteService() inside business logic→program to an interface; inject it through the constructor
  • Subclasses multiply: UrgentSmsNotifier, DigestEmailNotifier…→composition: Notifier(IChannel, IStyle)
  • A Manager / Utils class does unrelated jobs→low cohesion: split by reason to change
  • A rules engine, plug-in loader or option "just in case"→KISS + YAGNI: build the simple version; refactor when the need is real
  • Interview: "isn't that over-engineering?"→name the change you expect, and add the abstraction only for that

Rule of three: duplicate once if you must; extract a shared abstraction when the same knowledge appears a third time. Extracting after two look-alikes often produces a helper full of flags.

LINQ and fluent builders aren't Demeter violations: each call returns the same kind of thing you already had. The law is about reaching through other objects' internals; plain DTOs (dto.Address.City) are usually fine too.

YAGNI isn't "no design": tests, clear names and small classes make future change cheaper and still apply. Think ahead for decisions that are expensive to reverse: public API contracts, database schema, security.

The principles pull against each other. DRY and SOLID encourage abstractions; KISS and YAGNI resist them. Good design is choosing on purpose and being able to say why.

4Test yourself

Which principle is broken?

Explain it out loud

Answer before you open each card.

DRY is about duplicated what, exactly?Show answer

Knowledge: a rule, a calculation, a schema, a setting. Two blocks of code that look alike but belong to different rules are not a DRY violation; merging them couples things that change for different reasons.

Under the Law of Demeter, which objects may a method call?Show answer

Its own object, its parameters, objects it creates, and its object's fields. Not objects returned by those (strangers).

Rewrite appointment.Patient.Insurance.Policy.Plan.Copay.Show answer

appointment.Patient.CopayFor(service), or appointment.Copay(). The object that owns the data computes it, so callers don't depend on how insurance is stored.

Give two reasons to prefer composition over inheritance.Show answer

Subclasses multiply with every combination of variations, and a change in a base class ripples into every subclass (fragile base class). Composition combines small parts and can change them at runtime.

What do cohesion and coupling measure, and which way should each go?Show answer

Cohesion: how well the parts inside a module belong together: aim high. Coupling: how much modules depend on each other: aim low. Together they keep a change local and contained.

When does YAGNI not stop you adding an interface?Show answer

When there's a real need now: a second implementation exists, or a test needs a fake (an IClock, an IPaymentGateway). YAGNI targets speculative features and flexibility, not testability.
