Week 1 / H6 / Networking Fundamentals

Availability, SLIs, SLOs & SLAs

  • SLI = measure: what actually happened (e.g. % of requests that succeeded).
  • SLO = target: what the team aims for (e.g. 99.9% over 30 days).
  • SLA = promise: what customers are contractually owed if you miss it.
  • Error budget = 100% − SLO: the amount of failure you are allowed. The team can 'spend' it on the risk of shipping changes.

Why SLO is stricter than SLA

The internal target (99.9%) is missed long before the contract (99.5%) is broken. When the error budget runs out, the team slows risky releases before customers are owed service credits.

2Compare: recovering from a disaster (RPO, RTO)

RPO and RTO

RPO, how much data can we lose?

Recovery point objective, measured in time. "RPO 5 minutes" means after a disaster you may lose at most the last 5 minutes of writes. Set by how often you back up or replicate.

RTO, how long can we be down?

Recovery time objective. "RTO 1 hour" means service must be back within an hour. Set by how much is already running in the recovery site.

How they relate to MTTR

MTTR (mean time to recover) is the measured average time to repair ordinary failures. RTO is the target for a disaster. Lower RPO and RTO cost more: compare the four strategies below.

Series multiplies: a request needing three 99.9% components succeeds 99.9% × 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.7% of the time, worse than any single part. Redundancy adds nines (more 9s in the percentage). Two independent 99% copies give 1 − (0.01)² = 99.99%. This only holds if failover (switching to the healthy copy) works and the copies don't fail at the same time.

Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR). MTBF is the mean time between failures, MTTR the mean time to recover. Halving recovery time helps as much as halving failures, which is why fast rollbacks and alerts matter.

Two ways to measure: time-based ("up 99.9% of minutes") or request-based ("99.9% of requests succeeded"). Request-based SLIs reflect what users actually felt, including partial outages.

4Revise in two minutes

Cheat-sheet

  • 99.9% vs 99.99% per month→~43 min vs ~4.3 min of downtime
  • SLI / SLO / SLA→What you measure / your internal target / the contract with penalties
  • Error budget→100% − SLO. Use it on releases; when it is gone, freeze (stop) risky releases.
  • Components in a chain→Multiply: 99.9% × 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ 99.7%
  • Redundant copies in parallel→1 − (1 − a)ⁿ: two 99% copies ≈ 99.99%
  • Availability from failures→MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR): faster repair helps as much as fewer failures
  • How much data can we lose / how long down→RPO / RTO
  • RPO & RTO near zero→Active-active across regions (most expensive)

"99.95% of requests succeeded last month." SLI, SLO or SLA?Show answer

What is the monthly error budget for a 99.9% SLO?Show answer

0.1% ≈ 43.2 minutes of full downtime in a 30-day month, or the request-weighted equivalent.

Why not aim for 100%?Show answer

Each extra nine costs far more. Users can't tell 99.999% from 100%, because their own Wi-Fi fails more often than that. A budget lets teams trade reliability for speed deliberately.

Your API is 99.99% but depends on a 99.9% database. Best case for the API?Show answer

About 99.89%. Series availability multiplies, so the API can't beat its weakest required dependency.

The site returns pages fast, but with the wrong prices. Available? Reliable?Show answer

Available, not reliable. Availability is about being usable; reliability also requires behaving correctly.

Budget is exhausted on day 12. What changes?Show answer

Freeze risky releases and spend effort on reliability (tests, rollbacks, capacity) until the budget recovers.
